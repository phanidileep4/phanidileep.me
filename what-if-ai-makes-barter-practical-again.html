What If AI Makes Barter Practical Again?

A seven-year-old idea about exchange without immediate money feels newly relevant in the age of AI—but also more complicated.

By Phani Dileep · September 20, 2026

In September 2019, I created a GitHub repository called BarterSystem.

There was no grand launch. There was barely a project. The repository contained a two-line README describing a private proof of concept and, later, an empty test file. Then it sat untouched for seven years.

But the thought behind it stayed with me.

I had a gut feeling that some form of direct exchange might become relevant again—not because humanity would forget money, but because technology might eventually make it easier to discover what people can offer one another.

Today, with anxiety around AI and AGI growing, I find myself returning to that old repository.

The fear beneath the AI conversation

Some discussion about AI is exaggerated. Fear spreads faster than careful analysis, and dramatic predictions get more attention than uncertainty.

But beneath the fear-mongering are legitimate questions.

What happens if a small number of people and companies capture most of AI's productivity gains? What happens to workers whose skills become easier to automate? What if those who know how to use AI become dramatically more productive while others struggle to enter the new economy? What happens when the gap between owners and workers, rich and poor, or highly connected and isolated communities grows wider?

The outcome is not predetermined. AI could raise productivity and incomes broadly. It could help less-experienced workers acquire capabilities faster. It could also increase labor or wealth inequality when its benefits flow disproportionately to highly paid workers and owners of capital. Serious economic research describes several possible futures, not one inevitable result.

That uncertainty is exactly why it is worth thinking about alternative ways people might exchange value.

Money is not disappearing

I want to be careful here. I am not predicting that currency will become useless or that society will return to trading sacks of rice for shoes.

Money solved important problems. It gives us a common unit for comparing value, a store of value across time, and a medium of exchange that allows specialization. Without it, barter traditionally requires what economists call a double coincidence of wants: I must have something you want at the same moment that you have something I want.

If I can repair computers and need dental care, I have to find a dentist who happens to need computer repair. That search is inefficient. Money lets me repair a computer for one person, receive a broadly accepted medium, and use it to pay an entirely different person.

AI does not eliminate the usefulness of money.

But it may change the difficulty of finding the right exchange.

AI can see exchanges people cannot

A conventional marketplace asks people to fit what they offer into categories and keywords. A more capable matching system could understand needs described in ordinary language, account for location and timing, and identify relationships that are not obvious.

Consider three people:

A designer needs help preparing taxes.
An accountant needs a bicycle repaired.
A mechanic needs a simple website.

No pair forms a direct barter match. But together they form a loop.

A graph-based system could discover that loop. A language model could recognize that “help positioning my small business online” may match someone offering web design, even if neither person used the same words. An assistant could help clarify scope, availability, location, and expectations before anyone agrees.

This begins to look less like primitive barter and more like an intelligent coordination network.

offers + needs + constraints + trust
                  ↓
        explainable exchange paths

The critical word is explainable. AI should be able to say why it suggested an exchange and which constraints may conflict. It should not quietly assign a score to a person's worth or make binding decisions on anyone's behalf.

Value is larger than price

Part of what attracts me to this idea is that useful value often exists outside a person's bank balance.

Someone may not have spare cash but may have time, knowledge, tools, an unused room, language ability, transportation, mentoring experience, or a skill that another person genuinely needs. A retired professional may have expertise but no interest in returning to conventional employment. A student may be able to create a website in exchange for career guidance. A small business might trade unused capacity with another local business.

A good system could make these capabilities easier to see without pretending they are all interchangeable.

That distinction matters. Not every exchange is fair merely because both sides contain one item. An hour of work is not automatically equal to another hour in every context. Urgency, risk, equipment, training, physical effort, scarcity, and responsibility all matter. An algorithm may help participants reason about an exchange, but it cannot produce an objective price for human contribution.

The goal should be informed agreement, not automated valuation.

Resilience—or a shadow economy of desperation?

A barter network can be described as empowering: people turn underused capabilities into mutual value, communities become more resilient, and participation is possible even when cash is tight.

It can also become exploitative.

If people use barter because they have been excluded from stable work, the system could normalize unpaid labor instead of improving their position. Powerful participants might pressure vulnerable people into unequal exchanges. Reputation systems can reproduce discrimination, punish newcomers, or become permanent social scores. In-person exchanges create safety risks. AI matching can leak private information or amplify patterns of bias. Fraud, non-performance, illegal listings, harassment, and retaliation do not disappear because no money changed hands.

There are legal and tax realities too. In the United States, the fair market value of goods or services received through barter generally must be included in income. Calling something an exchange does not place it outside society's existing obligations.

The design test should not be “Can AI find a match?”

It should be: Can people understand the match, refuse it freely, protect their privacy, resolve a problem, and leave the exchange better off?

If those questions are postponed until after growth, the project will have misunderstood its purpose.

Reopening the repository

I recently reopened that old 2019 repository. I could have deleted the almost-empty history and launched something polished. I chose not to.

The timestamp matters to me. It is a small record of a thought that arrived before I knew how—or whether—to build it.

The repository is now public, but it is still intentionally early. I have added a clearer vision, an initial roadmap, contribution guidance, product principles, safety boundaries, and a set of starter issues. There is no production application and no predetermined technology stack.

That is deliberate. Before choosing a framework, I want to understand the exchange problem:

  • Which real situations would benefit from barter-like exchange?
  • When is direct matching enough, and when do we need multi-party loops?
  • How can recommendations remain explainable and voluntary?
  • What information is too sensitive to give an AI model?
  • How should trust, blocking, reporting, disputes, and incomplete exchanges work?
  • What would prove that the idea is useful—or that it should stop?

An invitation to think and build

I am opening BarterSystem to contributors.

You do not have to be a software engineer. This idea needs designers, economists, community organizers, trust-and-safety practitioners, researchers, and people who have participated in mutual-aid, time-banking, cooperative, or informal exchange networks. It also needs skeptics who can explain why the premise is wrong.

The open issues include documenting real exchange scenarios, defining a small domain model, comparing matching approaches, creating a threat model, and building an explainable prototype using synthetic data.

I do not know whether this becomes a useful product, a research experiment, or simply a better-understood question. Open source is a way to find out without pretending I already have the answer.

An old idea meets a new capability

Barter is ancient. AI is new. The interesting possibility is not that one replaces modern currency, but that the new technology may solve enough of the old coordination problem to make direct exchange useful in places where it previously failed.

Seven years ago, I left myself a two-line note in a repository.

Today, the economic uncertainty feels greater, the technology feels more capable, and the responsibility feels more serious.

Maybe the future of exchange is not money or barter. Maybe it is a richer system in which people have more than one way to make their value visible.
